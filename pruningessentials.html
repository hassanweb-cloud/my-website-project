Pruning Essentials

Regularly remove dead or diseased stems to encourage new growth and better air circulation.

Why We Prune

Pruning isn't just about keeping your garden looking tidy—it is vital healthcare for your plants. Leaving dead, damaged, or infected stems on a plant forces it to waste precious energy trying to sustain a dying limb.

By strategically trimming away these sections, you redirect the plant's resources to growing fresh leaves, blooming healthy flowers, and improving air circulation to ward off destructive fungi.

The 3 Ds of Smart Pruning

Step 1: Eliminate

Remove Dead Wood

Track down brittle, completely brown, or hollow stems. Cut them clean back to the healthy main branch. This prevents wood-boring insects from nesting in the dead tissue and spreading deeper into your plant core.

Step 2: Protect

Isolate Diseased Foliage

Look out for leaves covered in black powdery spots, rust lines, or powdery mildew. Snip these sections off immediately and throw them away—do not compost them, or the disease spores might re-circulate into your topsoil!

Step 3: Shape

Thin for Air Circulation

If multiple branches cross over and rub against each other, cut the weaker one away. Thinning dense center configurations allows sunlight and breeze to pass right through the heart of the bush, naturally keeping leaf dampness at bay.

Pro Tool Care

  • Keep Cuts Sharp: Dull blades crush plant tissue instead of slicing it cleanly, creating ragged open wounds that invite infection.
  • Sanitize Regularly: Wipe your shears down with rubbing alcohol after working on a sick plant to avoid tracking bacteria over to healthy targets.

The 45° Rule

Always prune stems at a 45-degree angle, sloping down and away from an outward-facing bud. This simple slant prevents water drops from pooling on top of the open wound, dropping moisture down to earth instead.
